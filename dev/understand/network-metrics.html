Scoring a simulated stream network#

A model makes a network of streams. A map draws another one. To say how well they agree, you need a number, and there are several ways to get one. This page tells what each way answers, and separates two uses that look alike: a diagnostic, which you read, and a criterion, which a calibration drives to a target.

It has no equation and no name of a metric. The equations of the downslope method are in Downslope distance between two networks. The names of the metrics, and how to write them to a file, are in [analysis.comparison] and [calibration.outputs].

The two networks#

  • The simulated network is not computed by a river model. The model finds the cells where the water table reaches the ground, and the network extends these seepage cells downstream, following the slope of the ground (What the model computes).

  • The mapped network is the network drawn on a map. In example 04, it is the permanent reaches of the BD TOPO.

A score never gives the truth about the model. It gives the distance between two drawings, and each drawing has its own errors. The mapped network holds the reaches someone chose to draw. The simulated one depends on the cell size, on the threshold that decides what counts as seepage and, in a run that changes with time, on the month you look at.

Four ways to compare two networks#

Way

What it compares

What it answers

Overlap of lines

The two networks as lines

What share of the length of each network lies near the other?

Overlap of cells

The cells of the simulated network and the cells the map crosses

How many cells do the two share, miss or add?

Straight-line distance

The same two sets of cells

How far apart do the two patterns sit, as the crow flies?

Downslope distance

The two networks, along the paths the water takes

How far must the water walk to get from one network to the other, in each direction?

Overlap of lines#

Both networks are treated as lines, with a small tolerance around each. A stretch of one network is matched when it lies inside the tolerance of the other, and missing or extra when it does not. The result is a set of lengths and shares, and the largest gap between the two drawings.

Use it to compare two maps, such as the observed network and the one derived from the relief. It does not read the model cells and it does not know where the water goes: a stretch near the map counts as matched even if the water leaves it in another valley.

Overlap of cells#

This way works on the cells of the model. A cell is simulated when the model puts water there, and mapped when the map crosses it. Then the cells are counted:

  • the cells in both sets (the overlap);

  • the mapped cells the model did not reach (missing);

  • the simulated cells away from the map (extra).

From these counts come two shares: the coverage, the share of mapped cells that the model found, and the precision, the share of simulated cells that are on the map. A low coverage says the simulated network is under-developed. A low precision says it spreads where the map has nothing. Two combined scores fold the two shares into one number.

One detail changes the numbers. This way marks every cell the map touches, so a line becomes a ribbon of cells. The downslope method draws the map one cell wide, like the simulated network. The cell counts of the two ways are therefore not comparable.

Straight-line distance#

Same cells, but now the question is how far. For each simulated cell, the code measures the straight distance to the nearest mapped line. For each mapped cell, it measures the distance to the nearest simulated cell. That gives two numbers, one per direction, each summarised by its mean, its median, its high values and its maximum.

A distance gives more than a count. Two simulated cells that miss the map, one by a cell and one by a kilometre, count the same in the overlap and weigh very differently here. But the distance is straight, so it ignores the relief. It crosses a ridge as if it were not there.

Downslope distance#

This is the method of [Abhervé et al., 2023]. The distance is the length of the path the water follows downslope, not the straight line.

  • From each simulated cell, how far does the water go before it meets the mapped network? The mean is the excess side: if the model makes too much stream, simulated water travels far to reach a map that has no stream near it.

  • From each mapped cell, how far does the water go before it meets the simulated network? The mean is the deficit side: if the model makes too little stream, mapped streams have no simulated stream to flow into.

The two directions are kept apart on purpose, because they tell opposite stories. The calibration uses the gap between them, in absolute value, as its cost. It also keeps their average as a diagnostic, and the three classes of cells that the confusion map draws: valid, excess and missing. The maps are explained on Comparison with the mapped network.

The average walk has a validity length. When it is longer than that length, the two networks are further apart than the map can be trusted to place them, and the score says little about the hydrogeology. By default the length is two cells, widened by the positional accuracy you declare for the map. A violation is a warning unless you ask for an error with on_roptim_violation.

Diagnostic or criterion#

The same number can be read, or it can be minimised. The two uses have different needs.

A diagnostic describes. You read it, put it in a report, compare two runs with it. It has no need to be symmetric or monotonic. The four ways above all work as diagnostics, and the overlap and straight-line ways are only diagnostics.

A criterion is a cost that a search drives to a target, over many trials. It must behave well along that search. For the downslope method, the cost is the gap between the excess side and the deficit side, and its target is zero. Zero does not mean that the two networks coincide. It means that the simulated network has as much excess as deficit. A model can reach it with a large excess and a large deficit that cancel, which is why the confusion map and the average walk stay on the table next to it.

Why not use a straight-line distance as the criterion?

  • A straight-line distance crosses divides. A seepage cell on the far side of a ridge counts as close to the map, when its water leaves into the other valley. The downslope distance follows the water, so it cannot cheat that way.

  • A mean straight-line distance has no sign. It cannot say whether the model has too much stream or too little, and a search needs that sign to know which way to move the parameter.

  • A count of cells moves in steps, and its best value depends on how the map is drawn on the grid.

Why not minimise the average walk, then? It is large at both ends of a parameter sweep, so it does have a minimum in the middle. But nothing puts that minimum at the point where excess and deficit balance. They are two different estimators. Use the average walk to compare two structures that are both balanced, and the gap to find the parameter inside one structure.

What moves a score#

Before you compare two scores, check that they were computed the same way.

  • The seepage threshold. A cell counts as seepage only above a share of its own recharge (seepage threshold). Another threshold changes the simulated network, and so every score.

  • The way the map is drawn on the grid. The rule for the mapped network (calibration.outputs.<name>.observed_rasterization) moves the number of mapped cells and where they sit. A one-cell gap can come from this rule and not from the model.

  • The cell size. Distances are in metres, but they are measured from cell to cell. A coarse grid cannot place a stream closer than about a cell.

  • The state that is read. A steady run gives one network. A run that changes with time gives one network per time step. A calibration can score one state, or two bounds: the complete network and the permanent one. A cell is counted as flowing above a visible flow.

  • The map. The permanent network of a map does not hold the temporary streams. An “excess” can be a real stream the map ignores.
