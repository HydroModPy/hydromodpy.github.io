Routing on the mesh: the descent D#

Where the water of a drain goes next is a question of routing. A mesh top has pits that a DEM routed on its own grid never had, and a pit holds the water it receives. Routing needs a surface on which every cell has a way down. The model top is not that surface, and raising it would change the ground the solver sees. The descent \(D\) solves this on a copy: the copy is filled, the receivers of every cell are read on it, and it never goes back to the solver. The drain elevations, the streambeds and the reach tops keep reading \(z_\mathrm{top}\).

The flood#

\(D\) is the result of a priority flood [Barnes et al., 2014]. Its seeds are the cells of the rim of the routable domain and the fixed-head cells (Fixed heads: the sea, the rivers and the sides). The flood visits the cells in order of their filled level, from the lowest seed up. A cell \(m\) reached from a cell \(n\) of filled level \(\tilde z_n\) takes

(35)#\[\begin{split}\tilde z_m = \begin{cases} \tilde z_n + \epsilon_m & \text{if } z_{\mathrm{top},m} \leq \tilde z_n + \eta,\\ z_{\mathrm{top},m} & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}\end{split}\]

The step \(\epsilon_m\) is 1 mm. When the run has a channel network (below), the step is 1 mm on the channel cells and 2 mm elsewhere, so that a filled flat climbs faster away from the channel than along it and its steepest descent heads into the channel.

The tolerance \(\eta\) is twice the drop under which the receiver graph sees no descent. With \(\Delta z\) the range of the top over the routable cells, in meters,

(36)#\[\eta = 2 \max\bigl(10^{-9},\ 10^{-9} \max(\Delta z,\ 1)\bigr)\ \mathrm{m},\]

that is \(2 \times 10^{-9}\) m when the relief is under a meter, and \(2 \times 10^{-9}\) times the relief above. A cell within \(\eta\) of the flood level is raised too, so that no link of the receiver graph drops by less than the tolerance of that graph.

A lake is crossed, never used as a seed. Its cells keep their top and are flooded to their spill level like any depression, so the lake drains through its outlet and the cells below a dam keep draining downstream. The role of a sink belongs to the walks that read \(D\): they stop at the first lake cell.

In the code: build_descent.

The receivers#

On the filled copy, the receiver \(r(n)\) of a cell is its steepest-descent neighbor [O'Callaghan and Mark, 1984]. Only the neighbors lower by more than the tolerance \(\eta_r = 10^{-9} \max(\Delta\tilde z,\ 1)\) m count, where \(\Delta\tilde z\) is the range of the filled copy:

(37)#\[r(n) = \underset{m \in \mathcal{N}_D(n),\ \tilde z_n - \tilde z_m > \eta_r}{\arg\max}\ \frac{\tilde z_n - \tilde z_m}{\lVert x_n - x_m \rVert}, \qquad r(n) = -1 \text{ if there is none.}\]

The filled range is a little wider than the range of the top, and the factor 2 of (36) covers that difference. The distance is between cell centers, so a diagonal step on a square grid is \(\sqrt 2\) cells long and wins only when it is steeper.

The neighbors \(\mathcal{N}_D(n)\) are the cells that share a node with \(n\) on a quad mesh (diagonals included, as in the D8 descent) and the cells that share a face on any other mesh. A seed keeps its steepest descent too, so that an accumulation passes through a fixed-head cell. The walks that must end where water leaves the model read the receiver with \(-1\) on the fixed-head cells.

Every link of \(D\) descends, so the receiver chains never cycle and every routable cell reaches a seed. The build refuses a \(D\) where a cell has no way down.

A pit, by hand#

A 3 by 3 grid of squares of 10 m, with a pit in the middle and a lower cell on the south side. The seeds are the eight rim cells, and a seed keeps its top. The flood starts at the lowest, the south cell at 4 m, and reaches the center. The center is below the flood level, so it takes \(4 + 0.001\) m.

The channel tree#

When the run generates a stream network (see Streams and the water mover), \(D\) follows it. That network was traced on the D8 pointer of the DEM routing. On the mesh, a cell that holds a pixel of it is a channel cell, and it is pinned to the next channel cell along the network. A pinned cell drains to its pin, adjacent or not, even where the top climbs along the channel. Without the pins, \(D\) would descend its own flats by flood order and run beside the channel. A cell outside the channel keeps its steepest descent. A run with geographic.river_network.enabled off has no pinned cell.

What D is used for#

  • Stream reaches and drainage routing (Streams and the water mover).

  • Accumulation of the outflow of each cell (Where the water table reaches the surface).

  • The flow direction figure, and the foot of each dam read by the heads rule of the stream criterion.

Both MODFLOW solvers build it at every run and store it with the mesh. A run stored without it gets one rebuilt on read from the stored top, without pins, fixed-head seeds or lakes, and the log says so. MODFLOW-NWT seeds \(D\) on the cells it writes as a fixed head, and builds no stream head and no lake.

The graph of the stream criterion#

The stream-network criterion (Downslope distance between two networks) does not read \(D\). It floods its own copy of the model top with the same flood, and reads its receivers with (37). The two answer two questions: \(D\) routes water in the model, the criterion graph measures distances on the top. They differ in these ways.

Both use the step \(\epsilon\) of 1 mm off a channel. The flood and the receivers always read the same neighbor graph: a flood that walked eight neighbors while the receivers read four would leave filled cells that spill over a diagonal the receivers cannot take.

Assumptions#

  • Water leaves a cell by one receiver (single flow direction).

  • The filled copy is a computation. It changes no head and no elevation the solver reads.

Limits#

  • Meshes without diagonals. On a mesh whose faces are not all quadrilaterals, the neighbors are the cells sharing a face, whatever the key asks.

  • A run stored before \(D\) was kept routes on a \(D\) rebuilt without pins, fixed-head seeds or lakes.

More

'D8' descends over shared nodes, the eight neighbours of a quad mesh, and is the paper: its distances are traced by WhiteboxTools on a D8 pointer, and the delineation that produced the catchment uses one too. 'D4' descends over shared edges only and departs from the paper. It is not a second-decimal choice: on a synthetic valley whose talweg runs along the grid diagonal, the most accumulated cell collects 100 per cent of the domain over shared nodes and 6.6 per cent over shared edges. On a mesh whose faces are not all quadrilaterals no cell has a diagonal and the descent walks shared edges either way; on a Voronoi mesh the two graphs are the same one.
